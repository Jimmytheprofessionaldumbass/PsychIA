Introduction
------------

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
In school, students are often introduced to dilemmas that challenge their
honesty. This happens most often when they fall under extreme pressure to perform
well in academics. Pupils very often fear failing and are willing to go to great
lengths to avoid bad marks, e.g. by choosing to cheat. This act involves potential
gains (e.g., higher marks) or potential losses (e.g., teacher penalties or
failing). Understanding how students evaluate these decisions is important
because cheating undermines academic standards. [1]
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
This study, therefore, aims to address the following question: How does framing a
cheating dilemma in terms of potential gains (gaining marks) or potential losses
(losing marks) affect students' behavioural choices, perceived risk, moral
judgments, and emotional reactions in a decision-making task? [2][4]
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
This question is important in the real-world context of academic competition.
Small differences in scores can greatly affect students' future options, e.g.,
choosing a school. The target population for this study is secondary school
students preparing for major exams like the IGCSE. The associated pressure can
lead to morally difficult decision-making. Understanding these choices and the
psychology behind them can help educators see how perceptions of gain and loss
influence students' thoughts and behaviours. [3]
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
Thus, this study explores theoretical and practical issues in students' risky
decision-making. The Prospect Theory is the framework behind this work (Kahneman
& Tversky, 1979; Popov, 2018). It demonstrates that people assess outcomes
relative to a reference point, not based on absolute terms. Individuals tend to
avoid risks in situations framed as potential gains but are more inclined to take
risks when the same situations are presented as potential losses (Balart et al.,
2022). This phenomenon is known as the framing effect, a cognitive bias in which
presenting information differently influences choices even when the outcomes
remain the same (Popov, 2017). [5]
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
Tversky and Kahneman (1981) depicted this theory using the "Asian Disease
Problem." Participants were asked to choose between two programs (in public
health) described with either a positive frame (lives saved) or a negative frame
(lives lost). In cases with positive framing of outcomes, 72% of participants
preferred the certain option, whereas in the negative frame, 78% chose the risky
alternative. These findings provided evidence that decision-making under risk is
influenced by how options are presented. This supports the Prospect Theory. [6][7]
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

Druckman & McDermott (2008) investigated how emotional states interact with
framing effects. [HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]They found that emotions significantly
influence individuals' tendencies to take risks and amplify or suppress the
impact of positive or negative frames.[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED] Importantly, the
specific effect of emotions depends on both the domain of the decision (e.g.,
life-death or financial situations) and the type of emotion experienced.
[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]These findings suggest that emotional responses play a key
role in shaping how individuals interpret gains and losses, which is highly
relevant to morally charged academic decisions such as cheating. [6][7]
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

Building on these findings, the present study applies Tversky & Kahneman (1981)
and Druckman & McDermott (2008) to investigate the framing effect in a realistic
academic context. By presenting students with a cheating dilemma framed as either
a potential gain (gaining marks) or a potential loss (losing marks), this study
seeks to determine whether students' behavioural choices, moral judgments, and
emotional responses replicate the risk-taking and emotion patterns from prior
research.


Research Methodology
====================

Research Method
---------------

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
This investigation was a laboratory experiment using an independent measures
design. This allowed for a controlled comparison of students' responses to
questionnaire questions between two framing conditions: a gain frame ("gaining
marks") and a loss frame ("losing marks"). This method allowed researchers to
manipulate and isolate framing (the independent variable) as the main influence
on participants' decision-making. Other confounding variables were maintained.
[8][9]
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

The experiment kept the same environment, instructions (see Appendix 2), and
time limits (3 minutes) for all participants, which ensured internal validity.
Unlike correlational or observational methods, [HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]an
experimental design gave us the ability to infer causal relationships between
the framing condition (gain or loss frame) and students' moral and behavioural
responses to the cheating choice.[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
This method is suitable for the research question because it aims to determine
how the framing of a dilemma influences students' risk-taking behaviour, moral
judgment, and emotional responses when deciding whether to cheat. The independent
measures design was chosen because each participant was exposed to only one
framing condition.[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED] This prevented order effects (e.g.,
learning, fatigue, or awareness of the experiment's manipulation) that could
occur if participants experienced both. It also reduced the likelihood of demand
characteristics, as participants were unaware that an alternative version of the
dilemma existed. Individual differences that could have skewed the data in one of
the IV groups (e.g., in morality, motivation, or cheating tendencies) were also
distributed evenly between the two groups, as participants were randomly assigned
to one of two framing conditions to ensure consistency with the independent
measures design. [9]


Procedure
---------

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]The sample included 19 IGCSE students (10 males, 9
females).[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED] They were all from the same school,
[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]recruited through opportunity sampling.[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
The research group had access to this participant sample. The subjects were
available within a time window for this study. Although opportunity sampling
limits generalizability to a broader population of students, it was practical and
allowed for timely data collection within the school setting.

This group was particularly appropriate for the research focus. The dilemma
involved exam cheating, which is a topic with personal relevance for IGCSE
students who face academic pressure. Their limited formal exposure to economic
theories reduced the likelihood of informed bias, e.g., participants changing
their behaviour because they knew the experiment's aim.

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
Following a standardized procedure ensured consistency. First, consent forms were
distributed and signed by the participants' parents (Appendix 1). Second,
participants were randomly assigned to one of the IV framing conditions using a
random number generator. As a result, there were n = 9 participants in the "gain
marks" condition and n = 10 in the "lose marks" condition. Participants from each
condition were tested separately. This prevented communication or between-group
influence. Participants then read standardized instructions (Appendix 2)
explaining that they would read a short scenario and then answer 5 different
questions. [10][11]
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
Participants were then presented with their assigned hypothetical cheating
dilemma using a standardized Google Form (Appendix 2). Each scenario described a
situation where an "A-grade student" faced a moral decision about whether to
cheat on an exam. It was framed as a potential gain (marks gained) or a potential
loss (marks lost).
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
After reading the scenario, participants completed a five-item questionnaire. It
was created to measure aspects of their decision-making. Items were rated on a
Likert scale (5-point: 1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree).
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED] It measured:

1) Likelihood of choosing to cheat
2) Perceived risk of being caught
3) Moral judgment of cheating
4) Anticipated guilt or regret
5) Confidence in (cheating/no cheating) decision

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
Participants had three minutes to read the scenario and answer the questionnaire
items.
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

Then, they were debriefed (Appendix 4). The true purpose of the study was
explained, including the framing concept and the rationale for measuring the
questionnaire responses, but also that the scenario was hypothetical and
anonymous. [HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]The experiment took place in a quiet classroom,
reducing noise and related distractions.[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[GRAY HIGHLIGHT]

  The materials included: [12]
[/GRAY HIGHLIGHT]
* The written scenario (gain or loss version) about cheating (Appendix 2).
* The five-item behavioural questionnaire (Appendix 3).
* Standardized instructions and response timer (3 minutes).
* Debriefing statement (Appendix 4).

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
Both scenario versions were identical except for the framing manipulation. The
gain frame highlighted marks that could be gained by cheating. The loss frame
focused on marks that could be lost by not cheating, in line with Tversky and
Kahneman (1981).
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]


Ethical Considerations
----------------------

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
* Informed Consent: Parental consent and informed consent were obtained prior to
  participation.
* Deception and Debrief: Mild deception was necessary to prevent demand
  characteristics (participants were not informed that the study examined
  "framing effects"). This was justified to preserve validity and followed with
  detailed debriefing information.
* Anonymity: All responses to the questionnaire were anonymous.
* Protection: All participants were informed that their answers to this
  hypothetical would not be judged, since the task involved a cheating scenario
  that could cause very slight discomfort.
* Right to Withdraw: Before and after the study, we reminded participants that
  they could withdraw their data at any point before analysis. [13]
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]


Data Collection
---------------

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
The data were collected using a Framing and Moral Decision-Making Questionnaire
developed for this study to address the research question best (see Appendix 3).
The tool included five items designed to measure participants' responses to a
cheating dilemma. Each item corresponded to a specific aspect of decision-making
under risk. First, an actual behavioural choice, a perceived risk, a moral
judgment, a measure of emotional discomfort, and a perceived likelihood of
success.
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
The inclusion of multiple items ensured that data collection was not limited to a
single yes/no response. It provides a better understanding of how framing
influences decision-making when choosing to cheat, which is clearly risky. The
Likert-scale questions allowed ordinal data to be gathered. This lets us make more
in-depth interpretations relevant to the research question.
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
Each questionnaire item explored an aspect of how framing (gain vs. loss) shapes
students' moral and risk-based answers when faced with a hypothetical cheating
scenario. The first item replicated the usual framing paradigm (choice between
certain and uncertain outcomes; Tversky and Kahneman, 1981). The other items
expanded measurement to include perceived risk, moral evaluation, emotional
response, and expected success, expanding on the Druckman & McDermott (2008)
results. These are important impacts on risk-taking in education.
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
Several difficulties could still become impactful during data collection. First,
differences in academic motivation, morality, or experience with cheating could
impact responses despite the framing condition. Random allocation and using a
sample of all IGCSE students helped reduce this. Moreover, cheating is a morally
delicate topic. Participants could, in consequence, under-report their
willingness to cheat because of social desirability. This was addressed by
emphasis on anonymity. The questionnaire was also filled out privately.
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
Participants might have also tried to guess the purpose of the study, which could
distort their responses due to demand characteristics.[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED] This
was reduced, as they were told only that the study was about "decision-making in
academic situations," and the word framing was not mentioned before debriefing.

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
As the questionnaire was newly designed, there was a risk of misinterpretation or
uneven item difficulty.[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED] Each question was reviewed by a
psychology teacher, and all items used Likert scaling to maintain internal
validity of the tool. Further, using a standardized Google Form minimized
researcher influence and ensured consistent presentation. [HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
Last, the influence of fatigue or failing to engage on our results is unlikely
because of the short task.[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED] A three-minute limit and clear
instructions helped sustain attention and prevent careless responding.


Discussion
----------

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
A predicted outcome is that participants who read the gain-framed scenario would
show a higher tendency toward reporting lower willingness to cheat (Popov, 2018).
This would be consistent with risk-averse behaviour in the prospect theory
(Kahneman and Tversky, 1979). Participants in the loss-framed scenario are
expected to display greater risk-taking behaviour. For example (in self-report),
higher willingness to cheat, increased confidence in cheating, and lower
perceived moral acceptability of refusing to cheat.
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
Likert-scale items on perceived risk and anticipated guilt would show that loss
framing increases the motivation to take action to avoid negative outcomes (even
if morally difficult). These results would provide support for the framing effect
in an educational context and extend previous research (e.g., Tversky & Kahneman,
1981; Balart et al., 2022; Druckman & McDermott, 2008) by demonstrating its
influence on students' decision-making and intentions.
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]


Implications
------------

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
The study could have an important meaning for understanding cheating. Educators
might consider that how they frame assessment outcomes influences students'
decisions to cheat or stay honest. For example, presenting feedback by
emphasising potential losses could increase risk-taking behaviour (i.e. related
to cheating). In addition, policies could gain an understanding of emotional
responses (e.g., guilt or confidence during cheating). They could be utilised to
promote ethical decision-making by highlighting personal consequences of
cheating, not just punishments and failures.
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]


Researcher Bias
---------------

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
One potential form of researcher bias in this study is confirmation bias. The
researcher may have influenced the wording of the scenarios or the questionnaire
items to align with the expected outcome (e.g., framing the gain or loss scenarios
in a way that makes the effect more prominent to the reader). This could affect
the internal validity, as participants' responses might reflect the perceived
expectations. Using standardized, neutral language in the questionnaire and
scenario presentation, as well as administering the task via Google Forms,
attempted to decrease this risk. Nevertheless, the potential for some bias is
still considered.
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]


Alternative Research Method
---------------------------

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
An alternative approach to explore the same research question could be a
semi-structured interview to study participants' reasoning in depth after asking
them the self-report questions. They could explore why they chose a specific
option, how they interpreted risk, and how moral thinking influenced their
decision.
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]

[HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
The value of this method lies in its qualitative nature. The questionnaire
captures measurable tendencies. In opposite, interviews would provide more
insight into the cognitive and emotional processes behind decision-making. This
could enhance understanding of how the framing of risky choices relates to moral
reasoning. It could show nuance that a Likert-scale questionnaire might miss. More
qualitative data could show individual differences in moral prioritization, risk
perception, or justification strategies, impossible to find in numerical data.
[/HIGHLIGHTED/CHECKED]
