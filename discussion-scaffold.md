> "A discussion of the potential findings of the investigation and the implication(s) for policy/practice…

> "A discussion of one additional method for investigating the same topic… we can gain a more holistic

## Structure to write into

His own note in the file says "each criteria/section should have 3 paragraphs". That maps cleanly:

### Part 1 — Potential findings and implications (≈3 paragraphs)

- **Para 1 — what each outcome would look like.** He is testing whether *perceived control* changes the
  magnitude of flow, measured by a classification on **absorption, timelessness and effort**. So the three
  outcomes to describe are: higher perceived control → higher flow score; no relationship; or an inverted
  relationship (too much control reduces challenge and therefore flow). Name what the *measurement* would
  show in each case, not just "it would be higher".
- **Para 2 — connect to the two studies already cited** (`Mazid2024`, `AINLEY2008109`) and to the
  challenge–skill balance model already in his introduction. If perceived control raises flow only when
  challenge is held constant, that is a boundary condition worth stating — it is the difference between a
  descriptive and an explanatory proposal.
- **Para 3 — policy/practice.** He already wrote the seed in a comment: "how it can potentially be used for
  making policies of the school". A concrete, small-scale example scores better than a grand one — e.g. a
  school practice of letting students choose their own task difficulty or working conditions, with the
  caution that the school should not treat flow as a compliance metric.

### Part 2 — Researcher bias (≈3 paragraphs)

He flagged confirmation bias in his own notes; that is one, and it is a good one. The guide asks three
distinct questions, so cover all three rather than one at length:

- **Para 1 — personal history.** Why flow, why this population: he is an IB student writing about IB
  students' focus. That is both the motivation and the risk.
- **Para 2 — relation to participants.** Opportunity sampling of 10th-graders from his own school, chosen
  by humanities grades. He is older than them; that asymmetry affects how they respond and how he reads it.
  His own comment already admits the sampling rationale was informal ("relatively blithe compared to us IB
  die-hards") — the academic version of that is a **sampling bias**, and it belongs here.
- **Para 3 — values and interpretation + one mitigation each.** Confirmation bias (expecting perceived
  control to matter), plus the concrete control: pre-register the scoring rule for absorption/timelessness/
  effort before collecting data, and have a second rater score a sample. Note what limitation *remains* —
  the guide's language is "how might it still affect the investigation even after that precaution".

### Part 3 — Additional method (≈1–2 paragraphs)

One method, not a survey of methods. The strongest contrast to his questionnaire/rating scale is an
**experience-sampling method** (repeated short probes during real study sessions) or a **qualitative
interview** — pick one and say what it would reveal that his tool cannot (e.g. *why* control felt absent),
then name its own limitation (reactivity, or the inability to compare magnitudes).

The report cap is **2,200 words for everything**. Measured: the current draft has **1,117 words of prose**
(comments excluded, figures/equations stripped), so there is roughly **1,083 words of headroom** across
Introduction, Methodology and Discussion combined.

strengthening Methodology and the Introduction. Do not spend the whole headroom here — the data collection
tool section and the sampling justification both read thin.

## Traps to avoid

- **Do not invent findings.** He has not collected data. The guide asks for *potential* findings, which is
  exactly why this section can be written before the experiment — but every sentence must be conditional.
- **Do not conflate the three bias questions** into one paragraph about "bias in general".
- **Do not add a second additional method.** The guide says one.
- **Do not let the appendix carry argument.** (That rule is from the EE guide but the same examiner logic
  applies; the tool copy belongs in the appendix, the reasoning here.)
